## 1. Overview

Assignment 2 requires a project or research proposal, presentation slides, and an oral defence. The proposal converts a justified research problem into a clear, original or useful, feasible, and ethically responsible research plan.

> **Working source of truth:** This document records the actionable requirements in the official Assignment 2 brief in a structured Markdown format. Requirements stated as official come from the brief. Recommendations added for planning are explicitly labelled as workspace recommendations.

Assignment 2 is a proposal, not the completed project. It explains:

- What will be investigated, evaluated, designed, or developed
- Why the work is necessary and significant
- What original or useful contribution is expected
- How the work will be conducted and evaluated
- Why the work is feasible within the available time and resources

## 3. Purpose of the Proposal

A project or research proposal is a concise and coherent account of the proposed work. It must define the central issues or questions, locate the project within the current state of knowledge and recent debates, and demonstrate the proposed work's originality, value, and feasibility.

The proposal also demonstrates the student's ability to conduct graduate-level research and communicate complex ideas clearly, concisely, and critically.

### 3.1. What the Proposal Must Demonstrate

The proposal and oral defence must convince the university that:

- There is a genuine need for the proposed research or project.
- The proposed work is significant and important.
- The work will make an original or useful contribution to the field.
- The topic is feasible given the available time, data, resources, equipment, supervision, and access.
- The research can be completed within the expected period.
- Ethical and safety issues have been considered and the necessary university approval will be obtained.
- The topic matches the student's programme of study, interests, and capabilities.
- The proposed methods are suitable for answering the research questions and achieving the objectives.

### 3.2. Acceptable Forms of Research Contribution

The project or research makes a worthwhile contribution when it does one or more of the following:

1. Provides evidence that supports or disproves a concept, theory, or model.
2. Contributes new data or information, a new or improved solution, an analysis procedure, or a new or improved project or research methodology.
3. Produces a new or improved concept, theory, model, framework, or comparable intellectual contribution.

## 4. Required Deliverables

### 4.1. Written Proposal

The written proposal must:

- Establish the academic and practical context of the research.
- Define and justify the research problem.
- State explicit research questions and any appropriate hypotheses.
- Present one overall aim and a set of achievable objectives.
- Define the research scope and significance.
- Explain and justify a suitable methodology.
- Present a realistic research plan.
- Use recent, reliable, and correctly cited sources.

### 4.2. Presentation Slides

The slides must:

- Cover the principal sections of the proposal.
- Present information in the required logical order.
- Use a professional and consistent layout and format.
- Include sufficient detail to support the oral defence.
- Be submitted online by the applicable deadline.

### 4.3. Oral Defence and Questions

The student must present and defend the proposal to the supervisor. The student should be able to:

- Explain the need, significance, contribution, and feasibility of the project.
- Justify the research questions, objectives, scope, methods, and plan.
- Interpret questions correctly and answer them confidently.
- Demonstrate deep knowledge and understanding of the subject area.
- Respond at an appropriate depth and in language suitable for the audience.

## 5. Official Proposal Structure

The official brief recommends the following order. Equivalent terminology may be used with lecturer or supervisor approval, but the order and terminology must remain consistent throughout the proposal and final project report.

### 5.1. Front Matter

1. Title Page
2. Abstract
3. Table of Contents
4. List of Figures, if applicable
5. List of Tables, if applicable
6. List of Abbreviations, Symbols, or Terminology, if applicable

### 5.2. Main Body

| Section | Official heading | Main purpose |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction | Introduce the general research area, significance, contribution, and proposal direction |
| 2 | Research Background | Critically review relevant literature and establish the research context |
| 3 | Problem Statement | Define and justify the unresolved problem |
| 4 | Research Questions | State the questions the investigation will answer |
| 5 | Aim and Objectives | State one overall aim and the objectives required to achieve it |
| 6 | Scope of the Research | Define the project's boundaries and expected extent |
| 7 | Significance of the Research | Explain the project's value, beneficiaries, and expected contribution |
| 8 | Research Methodology | Explain and justify the research design, information, methods, analysis, resources, ethics, and limitations |
| 9 | Research Plan | Present the schedule, milestones, tasks, and major deliverables |
| 10 | Summary | Conclude the proposal |

### 5.3. End Matter

1. References
2. Appendices, if applicable
   - Ethics approval or ethics-related documents
   - Gantt chart or detailed research schedule
   - Supporting instruments or materials

### 5.4. Alternative and Optional Sections

Alternative headings may be used when approved. Examples from the brief include:

- `Justification of the Study` instead of `Significance of the Research`
- `Purpose of the Study` instead of `Aim of the Research`

Additional sections may be included when appropriate to the project, such as:

- Research Contribution
- Deliverables
- Intellectual Challenges
- Ethics
- Resources

The order of the proposal remains important. Confirm optional sections, variations, and additional requirements with the lecturer or supervisor.

## 6. Detailed Proposal Requirements

### 6.1. Title Page

The title page should contain:

- Module name and code
- Project title
- Student name and student ID number
- Degree and programme of study
- Module lecturer's name
- Supervisor's name
- Submission date

### 6.2. Abstract

The abstract must:

- Be one paragraph of approximately 150-250 words.
- Summarize the entire proposal rather than only the study purpose or hypothesis.
- Cover the research question, theoretical framework where applicable, research design, sampling method where applicable, instrumentation, and data and analysis procedures.
- Accurately reflect information developed in the proposal.
- Be coherent, readable, and concise.
- Contain no information that is absent from the main proposal.
- Appear without a first-line paragraph indent.
- Be placed on a separate page immediately after the title page.

The official brief recommends writing the abstract last so it can accurately summarize the completed proposal.

### 6.3. Introduction

The introduction must:

- Introduce the recognized general subject area before narrowing to the specific topic.
- Explain how the topic relates to the broader area.
- Explain briefly why the topic is significant.
- State the expected contribution of the proposed work.
- Introduce the proposal's direction.

The end of the introduction may include a paragraph explaining the proposal's structure and how its sections connect logically.

### 6.4. Project or Research Background

The background section must provide a focused and critical review of literature relevant to the research problem. It should:

- Demonstrate knowledge of the relevant theory and research area.
- Provide evidence that the identified problem exists and needs to be addressed.
- Connect the topic and research aim to significant, recent, and current literature.
- Position the proposed topic clearly within its research context.
- Demonstrate awareness of similar and related research.
- Include critical review and qualitative judgement rather than only description.
- Establish what is known, what remains uncertain, and where the proposed research fits.

The review must remain bounded and relevant to the proposal. The supervisor should be consulted about the questions and boundaries of the review. The proposal background is not the final literature review; it should continue to be expanded and updated as the project progresses and new publications are found.

### 6.5. Problem Statement

The problem statement must identify a problem in the existing literature or practice that has not been adequately addressed. It should answer:

- Why does this research need to be conducted?
- What specific unresolved issue does the study address?
- What has not been adequately observed, investigated, evaluated, or solved in existing work?
- Why is this problem important?
- How will the proposed study address the problem?

The official brief recommends stating the main problem in one clear sentence at the beginning of the section, followed by its justification. The justification must use recent, strong, and reliable literature.

### 6.6. Research Questions and Hypotheses

Research questions must be explicit. Collectively, they must capture the full meaning of the main problem statement.

- A research question asks what will be investigated or what relationship, difference, effect, performance, or outcome will be determined.
- A hypothesis predicts a relationship or outcome and should be included only when suitable for the research design.
- Every research question must be relevant to the problem statement.
- The set of questions must be answerable using the proposed methodology.
- Each research question should normally have a corresponding objective.

A suitable introductory form is:

> For the purpose of this study, the following research questions are addressed:

The topic-specific research questions will be developed only after the final research gap and problem statement have been confirmed.

### 6.7. Aim and Objectives

The proposal must contain one overall aim stated in one precise sentence. A suitable form is:

> The main aim of this research is to ...

The section should briefly explain why the aim represents an important area of study.

Objectives must:

- Be small, achievable, and assessable units that collectively achieve the overall aim.
- Begin with action verbs such as `To identify`, `To design`, `To implement`, `To compare`, `To evaluate`, or `To validate`.
- Maintain a direct relationship with the research questions.
- Be supported by the proposed methodology and evaluation plan.

### 6.8. Scope of the Project or Research

The scope must define the extent and boundaries of the proposed work. Where relevant, specify:

- Target users, participants, or population
- Geographic region or deployment context
- Data, datasets, workloads, or cases
- Technologies, platforms, tools, or environments
- Type and extent of the expected outcome or deliverable
- Whether the outcome is a simulation, prototype, experimental testbed, or enterprise-level implementation
- Required precision, performance, or evaluation level
- Explicit exclusions and limitations

The scope should prevent unrealistic expectations about what the project will deliver.

### 6.9. Significance of the Project or Research

The significance section, also called the rationale or justification, must explain why the project is worth doing. It should:

- Describe the potential value of the study and its findings.
- Identify the intended audience and beneficiaries.
- Explain how the results may benefit them.
- State how the work will add to the existing knowledge base and/or help solve a problem.
- Explain the expected academic or practical contribution.
- Support the justification with recent and reliable literature.

### 6.10. Project or Research Methodology

The methodology must describe and justify the proposed methods in enough detail for the reader to understand how the research will be completed. It must explain:

- What kind of information or data will be used
- Where the information or data will come from
- What resources, software, equipment, access, or infrastructure will be required
- What overall methodology and research design will be used
- Why the selected approach is appropriate
- How the methods answer the research questions and achieve the objectives
- What ethical and safety issues exist and how they will be mitigated

The methodology should also address, where applicable:

1. Whether the research is quantitative, qualitative, mixed-method, experimental, action research, case-study based, design science, system-development based, or another justified approach.
2. The specific data-collection methods, such as experiments, surveys, interviews, questionnaires, observation, archival research, or library research.
3. Sampling or subject-selection procedures and why the selected sample, population, dataset, text, workload, or case is appropriate.
4. The instruments, experimental procedures, system-development stages, materials, and tasks.
5. How the results will be analysed and interpreted, including statistical or theoretical methods where appropriate.
6. Background and justification for any methodology that may be unfamiliar to the reader.
7. Practical limitations, possible errors, confounding variables, risks, and mitigation or alternative plans.
8. The evaluation criteria and measures used to determine whether the objectives have been achieved.

The brief recommends using a flowchart or another suitable methodology diagram with an explanation of each stage. Methods that cannot answer the research questions or achieve the objectives are not acceptable.

### 6.11. Project or Research Plan

The research plan may be presented as a timeline, schedule, milestone plan, table, list, or Gantt chart. It should cover the period from the beginning of the research to submission of the completed project report.

Include planned start and finish periods for relevant activities such as:

- Literature research and continued literature updates
- Required training or courses
- Research design and preparation
- Data, system, environment, or instrument preparation
- Experiments or investigations
- Data analysis and evaluation
- Writing and completing report chapters
- Supervisor reviews and revisions
- Seminars or presentations
- Final project report completion and submission

The plan must be realistic and should identify major tasks, milestones, dependencies, resources, reviews, and deliverables.

### 6.12. Summary

The official structure requires a `Summary` section but provides no separate detailed instructions for it.

> **Workspace recommendation:** Use the summary to restate the research need, proposed contribution, methodological fit, and feasibility without introducing new evidence or claims.

### 6.13. References

The reference list must:

- Include every publication cited in the proposal.
- Exclude publications that are not cited in the proposal.
- Use the correct APA referencing style.
- Prioritize recent and reliable journal articles, conference papers, books, theses, and other suitable academic sources.
- Contain complete and accurate publication information.

A reference manager such as EndNote or Mendeley may be used, but the student remains responsible for checking every entry and citation.

### 6.14. Appendices

Appendices are included only when applicable. They may contain:

- Ethics approval or ethics-related documents
- A Gantt chart or detailed research schedule
- Survey, interview, questionnaire, or observation instruments
- Supporting technical material

Appendices must support the proposal and should not be used to avoid explaining essential information in the main body.

## 7. Final Submission and Formatting

- Submit typed work only.
- Follow the general report formatting specified in the Thesis Handbook and the template available on the APU Knowledgebase.
- Use approximately 3,000 words, excluding references.
- Use APA referencing consistently.
- Revise, proofread, and edit the proposal several times.
- The submitted proposal must not be a first draft.
- Where possible, obtain feedback from a friend, classmate, lecturer, or supervisor before submission.
- Check Moodle for the current submission deadline and any additional submission instructions.

## 8. Presentation and Oral Defence

### 8.1. Purpose of the Defence

The oral defence assesses the student's ability to communicate and justify the intended project. The defence should demonstrate:

- The need and significance of the research
- The originality or usefulness of the expected contribution
- The feasibility of the topic, resources, data, equipment, supervision, and schedule
- The student's understanding of the topic
- The suitability of the research problem, questions, objectives, methodology, and plan

### 8.2. Official Presentation Structure

The official brief recommends the following slide order:

1. Introduction
2. Project or Research Background
3. Problem Statement
4. Research Questions
5. Aim and Objectives
6. Scope of the Project or Research
7. Significance of the Project or Research
8. Project or Research Methodology
9. Project or Research Plan

> **Workspace recommendation:** Add a title slide and a references slide where appropriate. Keep all terminology, questions, objectives, methods, and dates consistent with the written proposal.

### 8.3. Submission and Delivery

- Presentation slides must be submitted online by the applicable deadline.
- The oral presentation may be conducted face-to-face, online, or through a recording, as determined by the supervisor.
- Confirm the presentation duration, delivery mode, slide deadline, and any supervisor-specific requirements before finalizing the presentation.

## 9. Academic Integrity

Plagiarism means presenting another person's work, ideas, or writing as one's own. It is a serious academic offence and is prohibited.

To avoid plagiarism:

- Write in the student's own academic voice.
- Cite every idea, claim, method, result, table, or figure derived from another source.
- Paraphrase accurately rather than copying source language.
- Use quotation marks and citations when a direct quotation is genuinely necessary.
- Keep complete source records while researching and drafting.
- Check that every in-text citation has a matching reference and every reference is cited.

## 11. Alignment Rules

The following chain must remain consistent throughout the written proposal, slides, and oral defence:

1. The literature identifies the research gap.
2. The gap justifies the problem statement.
3. The problem statement is decomposed into research questions.
4. One overall aim states the intended research outcome.
5. The objectives define the assessable work needed to achieve the aim and answer the questions.
6. The scope defines the boundaries within which the objectives will be completed.
7. The methodology explains how each question will be answered and each objective evaluated.
8. The research plan allocates realistic time and resources to the methodology and deliverables.
9. The expected contribution follows from the problem, results, and evaluation rather than being asserted without evidence.

Use the same terminology for the problem, research questions, aim, objectives, methods, metrics, and deliverables in every Assignment 2 artifact.

## 12. Recommended Working Process

> **Workspace recommendation:** The following workflow operationalizes the brief. It is not an additional official requirement.

1. Confirm the topic and the specific research gap inherited from Assignment 1.
2. Write a one-sentence problem statement and support it with recent literature.
3. Formulate explicit and answerable research questions.
4. Write one overall aim and map one or more objectives to each research question.
5. Define the scope, beneficiaries, and expected contribution.
6. Select and justify the research methodology and evaluation measures.
7. Confirm data, tools, infrastructure, access, ethics, safety, risks, and alternative plans.
8. Build the research schedule and Gantt chart.
9. Draft the full proposal and verify alignment across all sections.
10. Write the abstract last.
11. Prepare slides that mirror the approved proposal.
12. Rehearse the oral defence and prepare evidence-based answers to likely questions.
